Artifact 1: The Evolution of AI, a Visual Timeline

AIML-500 Fundamentals of AI and ML · Workshop 1 · October 2026

Introduction

This was my first project in the AI program. We were asked to research AI's history from the 1950s onward and present it as a visual timeline. I'm sharing it here as a quick reference for software engineers who are starting to work with AI and want the big picture fast.

Description

The timeline is a four-slide PowerPoint deck. The first part covers the early years from 1950 to 1997, including Alan Turing, the Dartmouth workshop, ELIZA, and Deep Blue. The two AI winters are marked in black so they stand out. The second part covers 2006 to today: cloud computing, AlexNet, AlphaGo, the Transformer, and ChatGPT. The last slide lists the sources.

View PDFDownload PowerPoint

Objective

I wanted to understand how AI got to where it is now, especially why progress stalled twice before. I also wanted something short enough that a busy engineer could read it in two minutes.

Process

  • Read the course resources from the IEEE Computer Society and Our World in Data to find the key milestones.
  • Picked the events that changed the direction of the field, plus the two AI winters and what caused them.
  • Used Claude to help research the milestones and build a first draft in PowerPoint.
  • The first draft had seven slides and a lot of detail. It was too much for someone new to the topic, so I cut it to four slides with one short line per event.
  • Checked the events against the original papers and official sources listed in the references.

Tools and Technologies Used

  • Microsoft PowerPoint for the slides
  • Claude (Anthropic) to help with research and the first draft of the slides
  • Course readings and primary sources for fact-checking
  • GitHub Pages to publish this portfolio

Value Proposition

Unique value: It squeezes about 75 years of AI history into four slides in plain language, and it prints cleanly in black and white. Someone with no AI background can follow it.

Relevance: Both AI winters happened because people promised more than the technology could deliver. Teams starting AI projects today face the same risk, so knowing this history helps set realistic expectations with stakeholders. That fits the kind of engineer I want to be.
